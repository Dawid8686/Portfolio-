Introduction to Monochrome Photography in Luminar Neo

Converting a color photograph to black and white is a process that requires much more than simply removing saturation. The key to achieving a deep, three-dimensional image lies in managing contrast, micro-structure, and subtle toning. Luminar Neo offers both traditional slider-based tools and intelligent AI algorithms that speed up this workflow. In this guide, you will learn step-by-step how to master these features.

Before starting, it is worth keeping in mind the limitations of the software itself. Luminar Neo lacks advanced channel mixing with curves found in traditional advanced RAW converters, and AI engines sometimes require manual mask corrections because the algorithms might interpret a scene differently than your artistic vision intended. Despite this, the working speed and precision of selected tools make it a great choice for photographers at any skill level.

Step 1: Initial Conversion and Exposure

The first step when wondering how to make black and white photo in luminar neo is importing your RAW file and switching to the Edit tab. Before touching monochrome tools, you must ensure proper base tonal dynamics.

  • Open the Develop panel.
  • Go to the Black & White section and check the conversion option, or simply pull the Saturation slider down to zero in the Color section, though dedicated conversion provides a better starting point.
  • Correct exposure and tame blown highlights in the Highlights and Shadows sections. Local contrast is critical in black and white, so avoid flat, gray tonal transitions right from the start.

Step 2: Precise Tonal Contrast Control

Black and white frames live by contrast. Unlike color photography, where the viewer's eye wanders across hues, monochrome relies entirely on luminance. Luminar Neo offers several dedicated tools for this purpose.

Use the Structure AI tool, which extracts details from textures like stone, skin, or clothing. Avoid overdoing it, however, as excessive structure creates unsightly halos around edges. Set the value cautiously, within 10-20 points. Next, go to the Supercontrast panel, which divides the image into four independent zones:

  • Highlights: Allows you to darken or brighten the brightest areas without losing detail.
  • Midtones: Controls the main body of the image. Increasing midtone contrast gives the photo punch.
  • Shadows: Gives control over the darkest parts of the image. You can pull them down deep for strong blacks.
  • The Balance sliders for each section let you precisely define the tonal cutoff point.

Step 3: Color Mixing Before Conversion

Even though the final image is black and white, how the individual colors of the original RAW file are translated into grayscale is fundamentally important. Returning to the black and white conversion panel or using the BW Conversion tool, you will find sliders for individual color channels.

For example, if you are photographing a portrait against the sky:

  • Darkening the Blue channel will make the sky dramatically dark, accentuating clouds.
  • Brightening the Red and Yellow channels will smooth out skin tones and brighten the subject's face.

This is a technique rooted in traditional analog photography, where colored filters were placed on the camera lens. In Luminar Neo, you do this digitally using individual color luminance sliders.

Step 4: Toning and Character (Split Toning)

Once you have a solid, high-contrast black and white skeleton, it is time to give it a final artistic touch through toning. Monochrome photos are rarely perfectly neutral—warmth (sepia) or coolness (steel) is often added.

Use the Toning tool to separate shades:

  • Highlights: Add a delicate yellow or orange hue to simulate warm sunlight or old photographic paper.
  • Shadows: Introduce a cool bluish or cyan hue to create the classic contrast between warm highlights and cool shadows (split-tone effect).
  • Adjust the Amount slider for each zone very sparingly—overdone toning looks unnatural and amateurish.

Step 5: Using Masking and AI for Local Adjustments

The final stage involves working on the composition of light. The Vignette tool darkens the corners of the frame, naturally drawing the viewer's eye inward. You can also use the brush masking or AI masks to brighten a specific element (like the model's eyes or the main subject) and darken the background.

If you want to test these features in practice and see how AI algorithms handle your RAW files, download Luminar Neo and practice the steps above on your own landscape or portrait photographs.
